Brazil — Estratégia Saúde da Família

A fixed-territory, team-based primary care model that grew from a small rural pilot into one of the largest primary care systems in the world.

Since
1994, as the Family Health Program (Programa Saúde da Família); scaled continuously through the 2000s and 2010s
Mechanism
Fixed-territory family health teams — one physician, one nurse, one nurse aide, and four or more community health agents — each responsible for a defined population, typically up to around 1,000 households
Scale
Grew from about 7 million people covered (4% of the population) in 1998 to 120 million people (roughly 62% of the population) by 2014
What's documented as working
Systematic reviews have found reductions in mortality from conditions primary care is meant to catch early, and at least one national longitudinal study found the program's expansion was associated with narrowing racial disparities in some outcomes
Documented challenge
Real, documented unevenness in resourcing between regions — technologically well-equipped units next to ones with unreliable electricity — and continued reliance on the personal initiative of community health agents working under precarious employment conditions

Brazil's Estratégia Saúde da Família (Family Health Strategy, commonly abbreviated ESF) began in 1994 as the Family Health Program, built on top of an earlier Community Health Agent Program that had already been operating in underserved rural areas. Community health agents were formally recognized as a legal profession in 2002.

Its structure is team-based and territorial: each family health team consists of one physician, one nurse, one nurse aide, and a minimum of four community health agents, with each agent typically responsible for roughly 150 households and each team responsible for up to around 1,000 households. The program's scale-up has been described in the literature as remarkable — from about 2,000 teams and 60,000 community health agents serving 7 million people in 1998, to tens of thousands of teams and more than 265,000 community health agents serving 120 million people, about 62% of Brazil's population, by 2014.

Brazil operates the largest public health system in the world by population covered, with 120 million people receiving free primary care through this structure. A systematic review of the program's effect on primary-care-sensitive conditions found supporting evidence of impact, though the review also noted no full consensus exists across all outcome measures studied. A separate nationwide longitudinal analysis found that ESF expansion was associated with a narrowing of racial disparities in mortality from conditions primary care is positioned to prevent.

Documented challenges are specific and consistently reported across independent studies: real unevenness in resourcing between regions, with some units facing unreliable electricity and limited equipment; a program that in places relies heavily on the personal initiative of health professionals and community agents, some of whom work under precarious employment conditions; and persistent regional health disparities, with outcomes in Brazil's south generally faring better than in the north.
