Cooperative Model

The Roadmap

The journey towards making an effective cooperative model that addresses the needs for primary care a reality has six stages.

The following roadmap outlines what is needed at each stage to build the model—your model. The essential work is to establish the core competency, engage the right people, and create the structures that will support the intended outcome. Each stage lays the foundation for the next, gradually turning a shared vision into a durable cooperative primary care model.

One roadmap. A different route in every place.

The six stages provide the general path for building the model. That sequence holds whether the work begins with a community, an employer, a health system, or an entire region.

The specific plan emerges on the ground. It depends on who is ready to lead, what capacity already exists, which resources can be brought together, and what the local environment will allow. Those conditions determine the pace and shape of the work.

The roadmap remains consistent. The route and the timeline belong to the place where your journey ends.

The six stages

The first three stages build the foundation. This is also where efforts like this often die quietly preserved in a business plan that no one seriously pressure tested. The mistake is assuming, “We have seen this before,” instead of asking, “What must we look at differently this time?”

The final three stages turn preparation into build out. This is where the model moves off the page and becomes a functioning clinical resource.

Prepare — where meaning meets mission
Stage One Build real clinical confidence by defining your core competency

The oldest discipline in business planning still applies: walk a patient through the entire experience before asking anyone else to trust the model.

Begin with a defined patient population. Then follow the full arc of care—stabilize, treat, recover, reprogram. Start at the parking lot or bus stop, move through the front door, and continue from the first encounter to the intended clinical outcome. Do it on paper, for a specific patient with a specific presenting condition, long before a real patient enters the building.

Then do it again. Ten patients from that population. Ten different conditions. Dozens of passes through the full arc of care. Continue until the assumption that this is simply good primary care—already familiar and already understood—gives way to a clearer understanding of what the model must actually be able to do.

What is needed: Each walkthrough has one job: find where the plan breaks. Where does the handoff between stages fail for this patient? Where does the team’s instinct pull toward short-term relief rather than resolution? Where does the plan begin adding services or modalities without preserving the clinical arc?

The purpose is not to make the plan look successful. It is to expose where it does not yet work, determine why, and rebuild that part before moving to the next patient. A plan that passes through ten honest walkthroughs without changing was probably not tested. It was demonstrated.

At the center of every walkthrough is the model’s most important principle: healthcare exists only between the patient and the care team responsible for them. It does not exist anywhere else.

It does not exist in the building, the business plan, the technology, the billing structure, the professional credentials, or the collection of services being offered. Those things may support care, organize it, fund it, or obstruct it—but they are not the care itself. Care exists in the relationship through which a patient is understood, decisions are made, responsibility is held, and the full clinical arc is carried forward.

Understanding this is the model’s core competency. The care relationship must become the hub around which every other part of the organization turns: staffing, scheduling, facilities, financing, data, governance, and the use of each clinical modality. When those systems become the center, the patient and care team are forced to orbit the institution. That is one of the central reasons healthcare reform repeatedly changes the structure around care without meaningfully changing care itself.

Every walkthrough should therefore return to the same test: does this decision strengthen the relationship between this patient and the team responsible for their care, or does it pull attention and authority away from it?

Build the model around that answer. Test every system against it. Clinical confidence should be the result of this process, not its starting assumption.

Target Endpoint: Ten or more complete patient walkthroughs, each of which expose and improve a meaningful part of the clinical plan.

The result is a clearly defined core competency, a care model organized around the patient–care team relationship, and a clinical position that can withstand informed challenge because its weaknesses were identified and repaired before the model was presented to anyone outside the room.

Stage Two Solve the veil of statistics — before anyone asks you to prove anything

National health statistics were built to measure products and episodes — a drug's trial results, a procedure's cost. Cost-of-illness data measures the wrong population for this model entirely. Cost-effectiveness instruments are aimed almost entirely at products, rarely at delivery or continuity. A widely repeated statistic can look devastating and still mean something quite different from what it's used to claim. Every failed attempt this framework documents arrived at its decisive moment carrying exactly this problem — a case built on numbers that were never designed to prove the thing being argued.

What this actually requires: Stage One's walkthroughs address the core competency: healthcare exists only in the space between the patient and their care team. Understanding that this is where healthcare exists allows the plan to establish how to measure its own endpoints of success. This stage solves for the blind spot the standard of care struggles to address. This is the reason Stage Two exists — not simply because national numbers are misleading, but because a measurement system built around anything other than the relationship will always end up measuring the wrong thing, however sophisticated it looks. In practice: deciding, inside the plan itself, exactly what will be tracked and how, before a funder, a board, or a skeptic asks for it. Engagement depth over time, measured continuously, because engagement is the relationship made visible. Cost trend against a real, specific baseline — the anchor's own numbers, not a national average. Outcome measures that are both subjective and objective, because capacity is both, and capacity is what the relationship produces. This belongs at the same table where the clinical model and the fee structure get decided, because a plan that waits to design its measurement until someone demands proof has already lost the argument by the time it starts building one.

Target Endpoint: a measurement plan written into the business plan itself, standing on its own before anyone asks for it.

Stage Three Design the fee strategy as real numbers, not a diagram

The tiered structure — membership fee, copay, cash pool, mutual or major-medical insurance — is easy to draw and easy to explain in the abstract. It is a different exercise entirely to make it real: an actual rate, calibrated against actual costs, that a finance professional could evaluate rather than admire.

What this actually requires: a membership rate set against what care genuinely costs to deliver well, not against what feels defensible in a pitch. A clear answer for what fills the cash pool and how it is replenished. A defined scope for what the mutual or major-medical insurance layer actually covers, and where the line sits between it and the cash pool beneath it. And a test of the whole structure at the scale actually being planned — a fee strategy that pencils out for a hundred members and collapses at a thousand is a placeholder, not a strategy.

Target Endpoint: numbers on paper precise enough that Stage Four's anchor conversation can open with a real rate, not a range.

Execute — where preparation becomes a real clinic
Stage Four Find and sign the first anchor

This stage exists because of a specific structural problem: cooperatives have no equity upside, so conventional investor capital does not fit, and selling individual member shares one at a time is too slow to reach viable scale before the runway ends. An anchor organization solves this differently — it purchases primary care directly for its own population, at the rate Stage Three actually calculated, the same way it already contracts for any other essential service. This is the direct payoff of the first three stages: walking into that conversation with a defensible clinical case, a measurement plan already designed, and a real rate — instead of a pitch deck and hope.

What this actually requires: a case built on the anchor's own numbers, not a national average — its real, current cost trajectory, set against what direct care would cost instead. A pilot defined with precision before anyone signs anything: which population is covered, what the rate is, how long the term runs, and the specific measures — drawn from Stage Two's plan — that both sides agree will count as success. Legal and cooperative-structure work has to run in parallel with the sales conversation, not after it, so that a real yes doesn't sit stalled behind paperwork nobody started early enough.

Target Endpoint: one anchor, signed — a term sheet, not a running list of interested conversations.

Stage Five Hold the arc — the stage most plans quietly fail at

This is the stage that decides everything. Health care exists only in the relationship between a person and their care team — in that space, and nowhere else. Everything else this plan has built is infrastructure in service of that relationship, or it is overhead pretending to be care. The moment that priority slips — the moment administrative pressure, financial strain, or the pull to show results fast starts shaping clinical decisions instead of the arc itself — the model has not underperformed. It has become something else, and it will fail, whether or not anyone notices right away.

What this actually requires: protecting the arc of care as the organization's highest operational priority, above every other pressure a young clinic faces. Staying a coordinated hub even when it would be easier to add modalities to look comprehensive — inclusive care is the failure mode this framework was built to name and avoid. Holding the full arc to completion even when the numbers look good after one quarter, because relief is not resolution, at the population level any more than at the level of a single patient. The clinical team has to be willing to hold this discipline under real pressure, and the organization around them has to be built to protect their ability to do it.

What comes after traction, not before it: once the arc is genuinely holding — patients moving through real recovery, the relationship functioning as designed, not as a slide — only then does it make sense to sharpen the measurement plan Stage Two designed into something more precise: the specific outcome data, cost-trend tracking, and comparative measures that will carry into Stage Six and eventually into the policy conversation. Measurement refined before the arc has traction measures a plan. Measurement refined after is measuring something real.

Target Endpoint: a model that is unmistakably still itself — still holding the arc, still centered on the relationship — with real traction to show for it.

Stage Six Build the federated backbone — while seeding what comes next

This stage runs several things at once rather than in strict sequence: standing up the shared backbone no single clinic can build alone, seeding the next local cooperatives that will eventually connect to it, and — where the legal and financial structure calls for it — establishing an affiliated, legally distinct nonprofit arm alongside the cooperative to carry charitable and community-facing work the cooperative structure itself isn't built to hold.

A generic composition, not a specific design — the shape reflects proven patterns from federated networks already operating elsewhere, adapted to this framework's own structure.

What this actually requires, component by component:

Shared technology & data — one infrastructure for records, scheduling, and outcome tracking, so Stage Five's measurement discipline can actually scale across sites instead of living in one clinic's private system.

Mutual insurance & risk pool — a member-owned vehicle for shared risk, the same structural instinct that lets small, independent organizations self-insure collectively rather than each facing catastrophic exposure alone. This is where Stage Three's fee strategy — the major-medical tier specifically — eventually connects to something the federation itself owns, rather than an outside insurer.

Quality & credentialing — shared peer review and quality standards, so a patient's trust in the model doesn't depend on which site they happen to walk into.

Education & training — the mechanism that lets Stage Five's clinical discipline actually transfer to new sites and new practitioners, rather than living only in the founding team's judgment.

Advocacy & collective voice — representation that no single small, independent clinic could afford or command alone, but that a federation of them can.

A community foundation — where warranted, a legally distinct 501(c)(3) affiliate, connected to the backbone but structurally separate, built to hold the charitable and grant-eligible work a distinct nonprofit vehicle is suited for.

Target Endpoint: a backbone doing real work for more than one site, and at least the beginning of the next local cooperative it will eventually support.

The horizon

The institutional and policy conversation is deliberately held outside the six stages above, rather than given one of its own. It arrives only after all six have real substance behind them — because every failed attempt in The Record asked a legislature or a ballot for permission before any proof existed, and lost that argument every time. This model reaches that same conversation last, carrying an accumulated backbone and a real, multi-site track record instead of a request for trust. What it asks for at that point is different in kind from what Vermont, Colorado, and California asked for — not permission to exist, but partnership, support, and room to keep doing what is already working.

Timing a specific project against this map belongs to the people running it, not to this page. Every attempt in The Record carried a projected date into its decisive moment, and lost the argument when that date came and went unmet. The six stages above are built the same way this framework builds a patient's arc: toward a defined endpoint, not a deadline.

The one date on this entire page is not a pace commitment — it is the anniversary this whole framework has been building toward since before any of these stages existed. By 2038, the sixtieth anniversary of the Alma-Ata Declaration, the goal is for this framework to be fully public and actively in use — not as a marginal note, but as a demonstrated model of execution. Everything on this page is the path to getting there honestly, one real stage at a time, rather than one projected year at a time.
