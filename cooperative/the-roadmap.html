Cooperative Model

The Roadmap

How this model actually gets from one clinic to a national, federated network. Six stages, described in real mechanical detail — what has to be true, who has to act, and how each stage is actually done. Deliberately, no calendar is attached.

Why there are no dates on this page

Every attempt documented in The Record arrived at its decisive moment carrying a projection instead of a result — a promised timeline, a modeled savings figure, a five-year plan that asked for trust before it had earned any. Every one of them lost that argument. A date you have not yet hit is exactly the kind of thing a skeptical room is entitled to disbelieve.

So this page does something different. It describes what actually has to happen at each stage — the real mechanical work, the people who have to say yes, the structures that have to exist before the next stage can start — without pretending to know how long any of it will take. Some of these steps depend on a single conversation going well. Others depend on years of unglamorous clinical practice. Naming a date for either would be a guess dressed up as a plan, and this framework does not deal in those.

General map, specific project

This is the general path — the sequence that holds regardless of where it runs. A specific community, employer, or region turns this into a dated, resourced plan once real conditions are known: who the first anchor actually is, how much capacity already exists, what the local regulatory environment requires. The map does not change. What changes is how fast a specific place can move through it, and that number belongs to the place, not to this page.

The six stages

The first three stages are not warm-up work. They are where plans like this one usually die before they ever reach a patient — quietly, in a business plan nobody pressure-tested, rather than visibly, in a failed vote. The second three are where preparation turns into an actual clinic.

Prepare — where plans like this die before they start
Stage One Build real clinical confidence — not borrowed conviction

Not "we believe in this model." A defensible, evidence-grounded position that continuous, arc-oriented care produces better outcomes and lower cost — held with enough command of the underlying mechanism to explain it when someone pushes back, because someone will. Without this, nothing downstream survives contact with a skeptical room.

What this actually requires: a real answer to why continuity works, not just a claim that it does. The evidence that the therapeutic relationship is itself an active clinical ingredient — not a soft factor beside the treatment, but part of it. A working command of this framework's own execution-gap thesis: that healthcare's persistent failure has never been a shortage of knowledge, but a failure to deliver it consistently. And an honest accounting of what a real track record — Everspring's or your own — actually shows and does not yet show, so the confidence is defensible rather than borrowed.

What ends this stage: not enthusiasm. The ability to hold this position under real, informed pushback from a clinician, a funder, or a skeptical board member — and not retreat to "we believe it works."

Stage Two Solve the veil of statistics — before anyone asks you to prove anything

National health statistics were not built to see what this model does. Cost-of-illness data measures the wrong population. Cost-effectiveness instruments are aimed almost entirely at products, not at delivery or continuity. A widely repeated statistic can look devastating and still not mean what it's used to claim. Every failed attempt this framework documents arrived at its decisive moment carrying exactly this problem — a case built on numbers that were never designed to prove the thing being argued.

What this actually requires: deciding, inside the plan itself, exactly what will be tracked and how — before a funder, a board, or a skeptic asks for it. Engagement depth, not just visit counts. Cost trend measured against a real, specific baseline, not a national average. Outcome measures that are both subjective and objective, because capacity is both. This is not a data-collection afterthought. It is a decision made at the same table where the clinical model and the fee structure get decided, because a plan that waits to design its measurement until someone demands proof has already lost the argument by the time it starts building one.

What ends this stage: a measurement plan written into the business plan itself — not a promise to figure out reporting later.

Stage Three Design the fee strategy as real numbers, not a diagram

The tiered structure — membership fee, copay, cash pool, mutual or major-medical insurance — is easy to draw and easy to explain in the abstract. It is a different exercise entirely to make it real: an actual rate, calibrated against actual costs, that a finance professional could evaluate rather than admire.

What this actually requires: a membership rate set against what care genuinely costs to deliver well, not against what feels defensible in a pitch. A clear answer for what fills the cash pool and how it is replenished. A defined scope for what the mutual or major-medical insurance layer actually covers, and where the line sits between it and the cash pool beneath it. And a test of the whole structure at the scale actually being planned — a fee strategy that pencils out for a hundred members and collapses at a thousand is not a strategy, it is a placeholder.

What ends this stage: numbers on paper precise enough that Stage Four's anchor conversation can open with a real rate, not a range.

Execute — where preparation becomes a real clinic
Stage Four Find and sign the first anchor

This stage exists because of a specific structural problem: cooperatives have no equity upside, so conventional investor capital does not fit, and selling individual member shares one at a time is too slow to reach viable scale before the runway ends. An anchor organization solves this differently — it purchases primary care directly for its own population, at the rate Stage Three actually calculated, the same way it already contracts for any other essential service. This is the direct payoff of the first three stages: walking into that conversation with a defensible clinical case, a measurement plan already designed, and a real rate — instead of a pitch deck and hope.

What this actually requires: a case built on the anchor's own numbers, not a national average — its real, current cost trajectory, set against what direct care would cost instead. A pilot defined with precision before anyone signs anything: which population is covered, what the rate is, how long the term runs, and the specific measures — drawn from Stage Two's plan — that both sides agree will count as success. Legal and cooperative-structure work has to run in parallel with the sales conversation, not after it, so that a real yes doesn't sit stalled behind paperwork nobody started early enough.

What ends this stage: one anchor, signed. Not a list of interested conversations — a term sheet.

Stage Five Hold the arc — the stage most plans quietly fail at

This is the stage that decides everything, and it is not a verification checkbox. Health care exists only in the relationship between a person and their care team — in that space, and nowhere else. Everything else this plan has built is infrastructure in service of that relationship, or it is overhead pretending to be care. The moment that priority slips — the moment administrative pressure, financial strain, or the pull to show results fast starts shaping clinical decisions instead of the arc itself — the model has not underperformed. It has become something else, and it will fail, whether or not anyone notices right away.

What this actually requires: protecting the arc of care as the organization's highest operational priority, above every other pressure a young clinic faces. Not offering more modalities to look comprehensive — that is inclusive care, the failure mode this framework was built to name and avoid. Not discharging patients early because the numbers look good after one quarter — relief is not resolution, at the population level any more than at the level of a single patient. The clinical team has to be willing to hold this discipline under real pressure, and the organization around them has to be built to protect their ability to do it, not quietly erode it.

What comes after traction, not before it: once the arc is genuinely holding — patients moving through real recovery, the relationship functioning as designed, not as a slide — only then does it make sense to sharpen the measurement plan Stage Two designed into something more precise: the specific outcome data, cost-trend tracking, and comparative measures that will carry into Stage Six and eventually into the policy conversation. Measurement refined before the arc has traction measures a plan. Measurement refined after is measuring something real.

What ends this stage: not a date, and not a report. A model that is unmistakably still itself — still holding the arc, still centered on the relationship — with real traction to show for it.

Stage Six Build the federated backbone — while seeding what comes next

This stage runs several things at once rather than in strict sequence: standing up the shared backbone no single clinic can build alone, seeding the next local cooperatives that will eventually connect to it, and — where the legal and financial structure calls for it — establishing an affiliated, legally distinct nonprofit arm alongside the cooperative to carry charitable and community-facing work the cooperative structure itself isn't built to hold.

A generic composition, not a specific design — the shape reflects proven patterns from federated networks already operating elsewhere, adapted to this framework's own structure.

What this actually requires, component by component:

Shared technology & data — one infrastructure for records, scheduling, and outcome tracking, so Stage Five's measurement discipline can actually scale across sites instead of living in one clinic's private system.

Mutual insurance & risk pool — a member-owned vehicle for shared risk, the same structural instinct that lets small, independent organizations self-insure collectively rather than each facing catastrophic exposure alone. This is where Stage Three's fee strategy — the major-medical tier specifically — eventually connects to something the federation itself owns, rather than an outside insurer.

Quality & credentialing — shared peer review and quality standards, so a patient's trust in the model doesn't depend on which site they happen to walk into.

Education & training — the mechanism that lets Stage Five's clinical discipline actually transfer to new sites and new practitioners, rather than living only in the founding team's judgment.

Advocacy & collective voice — representation that no single small, independent clinic could afford or command alone, but that a federation of them can.

A community foundation — where warranted, a legally distinct 501(c)(3) affiliate, connected to the backbone but structurally separate, built to hold the charitable and grant-eligible work the cooperative entity itself is not the right vehicle for.

What ends this stage: a backbone doing real work for more than one site, and at least the beginning of the next local cooperative it will eventually support.

The horizon

One thing does not have its own stage above, on purpose: the institutional and policy conversation. It arrives after all six stages have real substance behind them, not before — because every failed attempt in The Record asked a legislature or a ballot for permission before any proof existed, and lost that argument every time. This model reaches that same conversation last, carrying an accumulated backbone and a real, multi-site track record instead of a request for trust. What it asks for at that point is different in kind from what Vermont, Colorado, and California asked for — not permission to exist, but partnership, support, and room to keep doing what is already working.

The one date on this entire page is not a pace commitment — it is the anniversary this whole framework has been building toward since before any of these stages existed. By 2038, the sixtieth anniversary of the Alma-Ata Declaration, the goal is for this framework to be fully public and actively in use — not as a marginal note, but as a demonstrated model of execution. Everything on this page is the path to getting there honestly, one real stage at a time, rather than one projected year at a time.
