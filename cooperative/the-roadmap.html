Cooperative Model

The Roadmap

How this model actually gets from one clinic to a national, federated network. Six stages, described in real mechanical detail — what has to be true, who has to act, and how each stage is actually done. Deliberately, no calendar is attached.

Why there are no dates on this page

Every attempt documented in The Record arrived at its decisive moment carrying a projection instead of a result — a promised timeline, a modeled savings figure, a five-year plan that asked for trust before it had earned any. Every one of them lost that argument. A date you have not yet hit is exactly the kind of thing a skeptical room is entitled to disbelieve.

So this page does something different. It describes what actually has to happen at each stage — the real mechanical work, the people who have to say yes, the structures that have to exist before the next stage can start — without pretending to know how long any of it will take. Some of these steps depend on a single conversation going well. Others depend on years of unglamorous clinical practice. Naming a date for either would be a guess dressed up as a plan, and this framework does not deal in those.

General map, specific project

This is the general path — the sequence that holds regardless of where it runs. A specific community, employer, or region turns this into a dated, resourced plan once real conditions are known: who the first anchor actually is, how much capacity already exists, what the local regulatory environment requires. The map does not change. What changes is how fast a specific place can move through it, and that number belongs to the place, not to this page.

The six stages

Stage One Prove it in one place

Everything after this stage depends on this being genuine. A single site has to hold the full clinical model — the integrated care team, the arc of care, the hub function — long enough that the resulting track record means something to a stranger, not just to the people who already believe in it.

What this actually requires: engagement depth tracked over time, not just visit counts. Cost trend documented against a comparable population, held to the same rigor a skeptical funder would demand. Real patient histories carried across the arc — the thing that makes the model different from episodic care — rather than survey data collected after the fact. A clinical team willing to practice the model as designed, and an administrative structure willing to track outcomes as seriously as it tracks billing, because the second habit is what eventually makes the first one visible to anyone outside the clinic.

What ends this stage: not a date. A record real enough that the next stage's conversation can open with evidence instead of a pitch.

Stage Two Find the first anchor

This stage exists because of a specific structural problem: cooperatives have no equity upside, so conventional investor capital does not fit, and selling individual member shares one at a time is too slow to reach viable scale before the runway ends. An anchor organization solves this differently — it purchases primary care directly for its own population, at a rate calibrated to what care actually costs to deliver well, the same way it already contracts for any other essential service.

What this actually requires: a case built on the anchor's own numbers, not a national average — its real, current cost trajectory, set against what direct care would cost instead. A pilot defined with precision before anyone signs anything: which population is covered, what the rate is, how long the term runs, and the specific measures both sides agree will count as success. Legal and cooperative-structure work has to run in parallel with the sales conversation, not after it, so that a real yes doesn't sit stalled behind paperwork nobody started early enough.

What ends this stage: one anchor, signed. Not a list of interested conversations — a term sheet.

Stage Three Build the capacity to actually deliver

A signed pilot is a commitment, not a functioning clinic. This stage is the gap between the two — standing up the real operational capacity to serve the newly covered population, which in almost every case means expanding inside existing infrastructure rather than justifying a new location before there is any evidence it's needed.

What this actually requires: an onboarding pipeline for the new population, built before the first patient arrives, not while they're sitting in the waiting room. Outcome and cost tracking clean from day one — this is the point where a shared data structure starts to matter even if a second site doesn't exist yet, because the numbers Stage Four depends on are only as good as the discipline applied here.

What ends this stage: the first patient, under the new arrangement, in the room.

Stage Four Let the pilot run long enough to mean something

The same clinical discipline this framework applies to a single patient's arc applies here at the population level: relief is not resolution, and a good first quarter is not proof. This stage runs until the arc has actually had room to complete for a meaningful share of the covered population — a clinical judgment, not a calendar one.

What this actually requires: tracking engagement, cost trend against the anchor's own prior baseline, and early outcome signal from the start, with a short, regular check-in rhythm with the anchor rather than a single year-end report that arrives too late to act on. The discipline not to declare victory the moment the numbers look good — because the measurement problem exists precisely because early, partial signals get mistaken for proof, and this is the stage where that mistake is most tempting.

What ends this stage: a real number. Not a projection — the thing every attempt in The Record never had.

Stage Five Prove it travels — the second site and the federation

One clinic proves the model works where it was built. A second site, in a different place, with different conditions, proves it travels — which is the harder and more valuable claim. This stage also stands up the shared backbone no single clinic can build alone: technology infrastructure, training, compliance, collective purchasing, and brand standards, while every site remains independently owned and governed.

What this actually requires: a federation fee structure and governance in place before the second site opens, not improvised afterward — funding shared technology, education and advocacy, a development fund for future sites, and a reserve. A governance model that lets two sites share real infrastructure without either one losing local control, which is the same tension every federated cooperative structure has to solve honestly rather than paper over.

What ends this stage: two sites, one shared backbone, neither site diminished by the other's existence.

Stage Six The wider network, and the policy conversation — arriving last

This is deliberately the last stage, not the first. Every failed attempt in The Record asked a legislature or a ballot for permission before any proof existed, and lost that fight every time. This model reaches the same conversation last, carrying a real, multi-site, multi-year track record instead of a request for trust.

What this actually requires: the federation's development fund and the accumulated playbook from every prior stage, used to support new cooperative formation in additional communities — each one moving faster than the last, because the path is proven rather than improvised. Only then does the institutional and policy conversation open, and what it asks for is different in kind from what Vermont, Colorado, and California asked for: not permission to exist, but partnership, support, and room to keep doing what is already working.

What this stage does not do: lead. It never goes first, and it never asks anyone to vote on faith.

The horizon

The one date on this entire page is not a pace commitment — it is the anniversary this whole framework has been building toward since before any of these stages existed. By 2038, the sixtieth anniversary of the Alma-Ata Declaration, the goal is for this framework to be fully public and actively in use — not as a marginal note, but as a demonstrated model of execution. Everything on this page is the path to getting there honestly, one real stage at a time, rather than one projected year at a time.
