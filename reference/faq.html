Frequently Asked Questions

The objections that come up first, answered directly — and routed to the primer that covers each one in full.

American primary care doesn't fail from a lack of knowledge — it fails from a failure to deliver that knowledge consistently. This model closes that gap, it's been tested for fifteen years in Minnesota, and the road to the rest of the country runs through Minnesota first.
Isn't this concierge care for people who can already afford anything?

No — concierge medicine adds a fee on top of insurance for faster access to the same system. This model removes the insurance intermediary and replaces the premium with the membership itself. Most members pay less overall than they currently pay for coverage that underdelivers.

→ Cooperative Model — How It Works
What happens in a real emergency?

This was never meant to replace acute and emergency care. It is the primary-care layer that reduces how often people need it. Insurance still covers catastrophic events — it just stops being the thing standing in front of a routine visit.

→ Primer: The Execution Gap — A Note on Acute and Emergent Care
Isn't this anti-science?

No historical clinical lineage is used here uncritically. Everything — from a century-old technique to a new drug — is held to the same standard: what problem was it solving, what was observed, and does it hold up when applied today.

→ Primer: On Historical Medicine
How is a monthly fee affordable to someone already struggling?

Because it replaces the premium, the deductible, and the surprise bill — it does not add to them. The comparison that matters is against what a family already pays for coverage that often still leaves them exposed, not against paying nothing at all.

→ Cost of Care: What Households Feel
Isn't "integrative" just a longer menu of services?

That describes inclusive care, and this framework names it as a failure mode on purpose. More modalities without a coordinating strategy is a menu, not a hub. The distinction between offering more and orchestrating toward an endpoint is the foundation the whole model is built on.

→ Primer: Three Models of Care
Where is the evidence, when it's one clinic?

Fifteen years of practice at a single site is a real, bounded evidence base, and the framework says so plainly rather than overclaiming. What would actually settle the larger question — a matched-cohort study, measured over years — is named directly rather than avoided.

→ The Measurement Problem
Hasn't cooperative health care already been tried, and failed?

Yes — and it's documented here in more honest detail than anywhere else making this case. Colorado put a member-owned cooperative on the ballot in 2016 and lost by fifty-eight points. That failure is named, not hidden, because understanding exactly where it broke is what keeps this route from repeating it.

→ The Record
Doesn't better, more attentive care cost more, not less?

It may, per person, at least at first — this framework has never claimed otherwise. The claim is narrower: the health that kind of care produces per dollar has never been counted, and once it is, the comparison changes.

→ The Turn
Why should I believe this actually happens, instead of staying a good idea forever?

Because it does not need anyone's permission to exist. It is not waiting on a legislature or a ballot measure to say yes before it can run — it is already running, and the path from here to the rest of the country is mapped, step by step.
